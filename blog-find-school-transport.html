How to find a school transport company the right way in 2026

Parents · 7 min read
Back to blog

Choosing who drives your child to school is one of the biggest trust decisions a parent makes, and it is often made in a hurry. A friend shares a number in a WhatsApp group, the price sounds fair, and the arrangement starts on Monday. Most of the time that works out. But the difference between a reliable, properly licensed operator and someone who simply owns a minibus is not always visible from the pavement, so it pays to know what to look for before you hand over the first month's fee.

This guide covers three things: the legal paperwork a school transport driver and vehicle should have, how a business's online presence helps you judge whether it is legitimate, and the places parents in South Africa can actually go to find operators in their area.

1. Check the paperwork: permits, licences and certificates

Transporting children for payment is a regulated activity in South Africa. A legitimate operator will not be offended when you ask to see their documents. In fact, the good ones expect it and will have copies ready. These are the essentials to ask about:

  • A valid driving licence of the right code. The driver's licence must cover the size and weight of the vehicle they are driving. Check the expiry date on the card, since licence cards must be renewed every five years.
  • A Professional Driving Permit (PrDP) for passengers. Anyone who drives passengers for reward needs a PrDP in the passenger category (often called a "PrDP P"). It is only valid for two years, and getting or renewing it requires a medical certificate and a criminal record check. An expired PrDP is one of the most common gaps, so look at the date.
  • An operating licence. Under the National Land Transport Act, operators carrying passengers for payment need an operating licence issued by the Provincial Regulatory Entity. It is tied to the specific vehicle and the type of service, so ask whether it covers learner or scholar transport and the vehicle your child will ride in.
  • A current roadworthy certificate. Vehicles such as minibuses and buses used to carry passengers must pass a roadworthy test every year (the Certificate of Roadworthiness, still often called a COF). Ask when it was last issued.
  • A valid licence disc. A quick glance at the windscreen tells you whether the vehicle licence is up to date. If the disc has expired, it raises the question of what else has lapsed.
  • A police clearance and child protection screening. Beyond the PrDP criminal check, ask whether drivers have a recent police clearance certificate and have been checked against the National Register for Sex Offenders and the Child Protection Register. Organisations that work with children are expected to do these checks.
  • Passenger liability insurance. Ask whether the vehicle carries insurance that covers passengers, not only the vehicle itself, and who the insurer is. A reliable operator will be able to tell you straight away.

Remember that these documents expire. A PrDP that was valid when you signed up in January may lapse in October. It is reasonable to ask the operator how they keep track of renewals, and to check again at the start of each school year.

Rules can differ slightly between provinces and change over time, so if anything seems unclear, your provincial Department of Transport or your child's school can confirm what applies in your area.

2. Look at their online presence

Paperwork tells you whether an operator is allowed to transport your child. Their online presence helps you judge whether they are a stable, accountable business that plans to be around next term. Anyone can print a business card, but building a real online footprint takes time and consistency, and it leaves a trail you can check.

Here is what a legitimate transport business usually has, and why each piece matters:

  • A Google Business Profile with genuine reviews. Search the company name and look for its listing on Google Maps. Read the reviews, not just the star rating. Real reviews mention specific things like punctuality, how the driver treats the children, or how problems were handled. Pay attention to how the owner replies to complaints.
  • A website or proper landing page. Even a simple site with the business name, contact details, service areas, schools served and pricing shows that the operator is investing in the business. Look for a registered company name and, ideally, a CIPC registration number.
  • An active social media page with history. A Facebook page that has been posting updates for several years, with comments from real parents, is far more reassuring than a page created last month. Scroll back and see how long they have been operating.
  • Consistent branding. The same name, logo and contact number on the website, the Facebook page and the side of the vehicle is a good sign. It means the business is easy to identify and harder to hide behind if something goes wrong.
  • Digital tools for parents. More operators now offer a parent portal or app with live vehicle tracking, pickup and drop-off notifications and clear invoicing. These tools are not a legal requirement, but an operator who gives you visibility into every trip is showing that they have nothing to hide.

A word of caution: an online presence supports legitimacy, but it does not replace it. Fake profiles and bought reviews exist. Use what you find online to build a shortlist, then confirm everything with the documents in section one and a face to face conversation.

3. Where to find school transport in your area

Once you know what to look for, these are the most useful places for South African parents to start searching:

  • Your child's school. Many schools keep a list of transport operators who already serve their learners, and the school office often knows which operators have a good track record. Some schools will also tell you which ones they have had complaints about.
  • Google Search and Google Maps. Searching "school transport near me" or "scholar transport" plus your suburb or school name brings up local operators with their reviews, photos and contact details in one place.
  • Facebook community groups. Suburb and neighbourhood groups, school parent groups and local "moms and dads" groups are full of recommendations. Post a question asking who other parents use and why. Personal experiences from parents at the same school are some of the best signals you can get.
  • WhatsApp parent and class groups. Your child's class group is often the fastest route to a referral, and it can tell you which operators already do the route you need. Treat a recommendation as a starting point, then run through the checks above.
  • Gumtree and Snupit. Many operators advertise on Gumtree, and Snupit lets you request quotes from local service providers. Listings here vary a lot in quality, so be especially careful to verify documents.
  • Hellopeter. Before you commit, search the company name on Hellopeter to see whether other customers have lodged complaints and how the business responded.
  • The LiftMe company finder. If an operator tells you they use LiftMe, you can look them up by company or school name with our Find my company search and go straight to their branded parent portal.

Red flags to watch out for

  • The driver or owner avoids showing you their PrDP, operating licence or roadworthy certificate.
  • Large upfront payments in cash with no written agreement or invoice.
  • More children than seats, or children sharing seatbelts.
  • No fixed business name, or a different phone number every time you are in contact.
  • No way to reach anyone other than the driver if something goes wrong on the road.
  • An online presence that only appeared recently and has no real reviews.

Questions to ask before you sign up

  • Can I see the driver's licence, PrDP and your operating licence?
  • When was the vehicle's last roadworthy test?
  • Have your drivers had police clearance and child protection register checks?
  • What insurance do you carry for passengers?
  • Who drives if the usual driver is sick, and are they licensed in the same way?
  • How will you let me know about delays, route changes or when my child has been picked up and dropped off?
  • Do you provide a written agreement and monthly invoices?

The right operator is happy to be checked

The single best sign of a trustworthy school transport company is how it responds when you ask questions. A reliable operator keeps their paperwork current, shows it without fuss, communicates clearly and makes it easy for you to see what is happening on every trip. Taking an hour to check before you commit is a small price for knowing that the person driving your child is exactly who they say they are.
